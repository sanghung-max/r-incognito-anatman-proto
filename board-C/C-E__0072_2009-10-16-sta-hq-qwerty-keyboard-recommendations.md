## 亂語 / text

### Perplexity | August 2026 | Report Summary

Samsung Telecommunications America’s 2009 _Mobile QWERTY Keyboard Best Practice Final Research Report_ establishes a research-based design framework for physical mobile QWERTY keyboards. Its goal was to identify physical keyboard characteristics associated with faster, more accurate, easier, and more satisfying text entry, then translate those findings into practical design recommendations and a product-development process. The report combines consumer usability evaluations of 11 devices, three focus groups totaling 27 participants, a performance study of 12 devices involving 119 participants, competitive-device analysis, and existing research on perception, psychophysics, hand function, and ergonomics.

The report evaluates a diverse set of portrait and landscape QWERTY phones, including the BlackBerry Bold, HTC Fuze, LG Lotus, Palm Pre, HTC G1, Nokia N97, Samsung Gravity, Samsung Jack, Samsung Impression, Samsung Exclaim, and others. It analyzes typing speed, accuracy, ease of typing, and overall satisfaction against physical variables such as spacebar width, key travel, gap height and width, vertical center-to-center key distance, number of rows, maximum actuation force, curvature, and tactile-response slope. A conservative partial least squares regression ranked spacebar width as the most influential factor, followed by travel, vertical and horizontal gaps, vertical key spacing, number of rows, actuation force, curvature, and tactile slope

The central recommendation is that the keyboard should be designed around a broad, centrally located spacebar. It should be at least 14 mm wide; for portrait keyboards, it should ideally span approximately three regular keys, while landscape keyboards should span at least two. The spacebar should lie on the device’s center line, function as a spatial anchor for two-thumb typing, and provide reliable tactile feedback across its surface. This recommendation is linked to Fitts’ Law: a larger, easier-to-locate target reduces the attentional effort and movement time required to reach it

The report recommends short but not excessively short key travel—approximately 0.011 to 0.014 inches—to balance typing ease, speed, and accuracy. Larger travel requires more work because activation involves force over displacement; extremely low travel and force, however, may reduce tactile confidence and accuracy. Maximum trip force should be approximately 200 gf (1.96 N) or lower. Keys should also provide a tactile-response slope of roughly 980–1100 g/mm, based on the performance of the better-rated devices

Key gaps should generally be minimized because gaps reduce available key surface and thus shrink the effective target size. The recommended vertical gap is 0–1.0 mm for landscape keyboards and near zero for portrait designs; horizontal gaps should be near zero for portrait keyboards and no more than about 1.2 mm for standard landscape layouts. Exceptions are allowed when curvilinear rows or visual separators improve spatial orientation. The larger design principle is to retain large target areas while using curvature, ridges, or domes to distinguish adjacent keys tactually.

For layout, the report recommends four or five rows, with five-row landscape arrangements described as more “fault-tolerant.” Portrait keyboards should typically have four rows and roughly 35–37 keys. Vertical center-to-center distance should generally be 6–7 mm, with 4 mm treated as a psychophysical lower bound based on fingertip two-point discrimination. Portrait devices should use strongly curved keys or customized micro-geometry; landscape designs should use at least moderately curved keys. BlackBerry Bold and Tour are treated as exemplary because their asymmetric, sweeping key contours support both two-thumb and one-hand typing.

The report also addresses key mapping and materials: frequently used punctuation should be accessible without unnecessary mode changes, symbols should be consistently grouped, number keys should be visually distinguishable, and surfaces should be matte rather than glossy or slippery. It proposes a formal development-and-certification process involving early concept evaluation, ergonomic compliance checks, heuristic review, performance testing, competitive benchmarking, and usability certification.

Importantly, the report cautions that its separate portrait and landscape regression models are based on only 12 devices with many co-varying physical variables. Its recommendations are therefore design guidance, not universal laws; designs outside the ranges may still be accepted following targeted usability research.
