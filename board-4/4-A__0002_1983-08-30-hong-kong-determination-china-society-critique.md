### 國事故人 | 1983
### 關於關永祥和洪逸生的《香港五百萬人自決前途》的兩點商榷  

**國事故人**


第四期的《學苑》,用第一版的位置以及顯眼的字體,刊登了關永祥和洪逸生兩位同學的《香港五百萬人自決前途》這篇文章。如果《學苑》編輯這樣做,是為了對作者發表文章的勇氣表示嘉許的話,還勉强可以說得過去,因為洪關二君在文中公開鞭韃中英政府, 也的確需要一份勇毅———————但願不是一時衝動!可惜這篇文章的內容,與它醒目特出的「包裝」很不對稱。作者對事物的觀點和分析過於簡單化,煽情有餘, 以理服人則遠遠不足,現在把文中值得商榷的兩大地方,分別討論如下。
#### 港人自決可行嗎?
第一,作者提出「香港五百萬人自決前途」這個政治主張,但竟然完全沒有分析一下這個主張的可行性,更加沒有提及落實這個主張的一些想法。當然 ,對於一篇不足二千字的文章,沒有人會期望關洪二君會詳細討論上述兩個問題,但他們應該明白到這個主張,在不少人的心目中,是一個近乎空想,毫不實際的主張。在這種情况下,對於港人自決的可行性以及落實的方法完全避而不談,無疑是一個十分嚴重的缺失。如果作者不回答上述兩大問題,縱使力竭聲嘶的呼籲港人自決前途,那又有甚麼實際的意義呢?

關於香港未來的政治安排,中英雙方政府當然應該要尊重本地五百萬居民的意願。因為任何安排都會直接影響這五百萬人的幸福。在各種考慮因素之中,香港居民的意願原該享有優先位置。 這是信仰民主自由的人視為天經地義之事。但另一個方面,如果我們是抱着實是求是的態度的話,卻不能不承認香港未來的政治安排,在現時及可見的將來是無法由本地居民來主宰。這不是潑冷水,也不是骨頭輕,說沒有志氣的說話 ,而是有根有據的論斷。
#### 港人的政治文化
首先,對政治冷感和恐懼的心態還普遍而根深蒂固的存在着廣大的市民中間、市民大眾還未醒覺到在未來的政治安排上,自己享有發言權。更加談不上他們有甚麼決心去維護,自己喜愛的生活方式。九七對絕大部份的市民來說, 可能是失業和不景氣的「代名詞」;是一個遙遠而抽象的問題;是一個莫名的恐懼;是一個茶餘飯後的笑柄。總之, 不是一個要同心協力去面對和回應的危機,大概是大半年前,兩電在百物騰貴 ,民生艱難的情况下宣佈加價,有一些壓力團體組成「各界監管公共事業聯委會」,發起「凍結兩電加價」簽名運動 ,得到五十多萬個簽名,但當局不為所「動,依舊我行我素。當局的反應這樣, 理由是很簡單的,這五十多萬的簽名眞的只是五十多萬簽名咁簡單,並不包涵有任何進一步行動的決心,所以對執政者絕對不能構成真正的威脅。要主宰自己未來的命運,我們要付出的代價又點止簽名咁簡單!如果我們肯誠實地面對自己,在政治文化的發展上,我們還是處於「飲奶水」的階段!

政治文化的轉變不是朝夕之事,要港人起而爭取自己的權利,確是「路漫漫其修遠兮」!
#### 政治架構的限制
除了政治文化這個條件不足之外, 香港的政治結構是另一個限制。我們根本就沒有這樣一個的制度:在重要的公眾事務上,能夠反映,判斷和演繹各階層人士的意向,並且使政府在決策的過程中,必須考慮各階層人士的需求利益 ,在尊重每個人的權利的原則下,以大多數人的意向為依歸。說得更明確一點 :由於我們缺乏一個由選舉產生的公眾代表所組成的立法機構,在香港未來政治安排這個涉及每個市民權益的大問題上,根本就沒有任何人足以代表香港市民發言和作出判斷。行政立決兩局的議員可以嗎?不可以。這個不單單是因為兩局議員全部是由政府所委任的,而且大多數是不屬於低下階層的,更重要的
是由於兩局議員一貫的表現是,在重要問題上,附和政府多於為民請命。那麼 ,中國人大和政協的香港代表又可以嗎 ?當然不可以。我相信十個普通市民之中,頂多只有兩三個知道人大和政協是甚麼組織,這又如何可以談得上,這些 「代表們」真的可以代表我們呢?

再者,在香港前途這問題上,市民的意向可能十分之分歧。一方面固然有企業家如李鵬飛等反對港人治港,另一方面又何嘗沒有持相反論調的安子介等人。一方面有南華早報、信報和東方日、 報所演繹的市民意願,但另一方面又何嘗沒有文滙大公所表達的市民心聲。各個社會團體所做過的調查的結果,也沒有顯示出在香港未來政治安排及與之有關的重要問題上,市民的意向是統一的 、面對香港前途這樣複雜的問題,我們現存的政治結構顯得十分之無能,無法平衡各階層人士的需求和利益!

最後,我們還要考慮時間上的限制大抵上,中、英雙方在明年底或以前便要達成協議,因為再拖下去,已經搖搖欲墜的港人信心,便很難再維持下去了。在這樣急促的時間之中,香港人的政治文化和政治結構不可能有本質上的改變。况且採取任何行動的時候,必須要維持本地社會的整合和穩定,避免引起廣泛的恐懼和不安。

以上的分析,雖然只是局限在香港社會內部的因素這個方面,完全沒有觸及中國政府收回香港主權的決心那個關鍵性的問題,但我認為已經足夠地顯示出,香港五百萬人自決前途這個主張, 在現實政治上是IQ零蛋,毫不實際的想法。
#### 毋須過份無奈 組織大聯盟
雖然港人自決是個空想,但我絕對不認為我們的命運是完全任由別人來擺佈。無論限制有多少,困難有幾大,也總有一定的餘地讓我們發揮影響力—————— 無論影響力是如何的有限,我們還未去到完全無可奈何的地步。華樂庭先生會經提出要搞一個由各工商、文化、宗教和社會團體所組成的大聯盟。表達港人意願。這是一個在目前各種限制下,比較可行的方法。學生會或者學界也可以想想發起搞一個各界關注香港前途聯合會議之類的組織。對於五百萬人的幸福有所裨益的事情,應該當仁不讓,挺身而出。
#### 關洪二君無的放矢
港人自決的可行性談過了,以下要和關永祥、洪逸生兩位同學討論另一個問題。他們說:「國家、民族,掛在我們口中,不過是一些空泛的名詞。我不相信、更加不會接受因為所謂「國家前途」「民族利益」,便可以捐棄一己的尊嚴。我可以決定以「國家前途」、「
民族利益」為自己的抉擇導向,但却不可以以此來強壓在他人頭上。人的尊嚴,乃建立於人可以為自己來決定、爭取自己的需要,沒有其他人可以代替他自己來決定,假若有的話,這便是壓迫。

作者認為人們可以用「國家前途」、 「民族利益」作為自己的抉擇導向,但不可以把這些價值取向强加在其他人頭上,否則,就是壓迫。如果有人硬要把自己的價值取向强加在别人身上,要别人接受,那當然是不道德的———————是蔑視人的尊嚴的表現。但這行為之所以不道德,是不在乎那强力於他人身上的價值取向的內容。只要是强加在别人身上的 ,即使是「反對國家民族利益」也同樣是不道德的。那麼,為甚麼關洪二君單單要指出不要把「國家前途」、「民族利益」强加在别人身上呢?是不是大部份提倡從國家民族利益出發,看待香港前途的人,都是把自己的價值取向、是非標準强加在别人身上呢?如果不是, 那麼關洪兩位同學上述那番說話就是無的放矢。如果是,那麼關洪二君顯然是歪曲事實,說話不負責任,或者是思路紊亂,概念混淆不清所致。為甚麼這樣說呢?

第一,在香港這樣自由的社會,對於香港未來的政治安排,每個人都有權根據自己的價值標準作出抉擇,以及呼籲別人跟從自己的標準。關洪二君大概不會反對這條命題吧!對於個人的問題 ,例如對食物的選擇,各人當然是依循個人的愛惡,我行我素,犯不着要人家也跟隨自己的喜好。但是,一涉及到人與人之間的事情,譬如說怎樣相處合作 ,而各人的價值標準又不一致的話,那麼要根據甲的標準還是乙的標準?這樣 ,人們之間就難免要互相勸服對方認同自己的一套,或者互相讓步,或者邀請第三者作出訟裁。這幾種處理方式都是自然而合情合理的。香港未來的政治安排,既然不是純粹個人的問題,大家的看法又不一致,那麼,各人呼籲和游說别人去認同自己的一套,又有何不妥呢 ?無論這一套是國家民族利益、個人利益抑或是大英帝國利益,這樣做並無甚麼不對。
第二,除非是運用暴力或者威脅用暴力,否則,運用合法的手段;譬如說在報刊上發表意見,在集會上講話,去宣揚「民族利益」、「國家前途」等價值觀念,根本就不存在着强壓和壓迫的問題。
既然在香港前途上,宣揚民族統一 、國家利益並無不可,又無從把這些價值取向强壓别人身上,關洪二君上述那番說話,甚麼「我可以決定以「國家前 』、『民族利益」為自己的抉擇導向, 但卻不可以以此來強壓在他人頭上………… ……假若有的話,這便是壓迫。」又有甚麼現實的意義呢?

如果關洪二君是指責是針對學生會幹事會和評議會,那麼問題的關鍵是在於他們的決定是否在憲章所賦予的權力範圍之內,並且通過合法的程序。在這種情况下,決定的結果是無關重要的。 無論是贊成抑或反對中國收回香港主權 ,如果不是經過合法的程序,一概是强加在同學身上的。這樣說,關洪二君的責難同樣是無的放矢,因為問題不在乎贊成民族國家利益至上與否,而是在於幹事會和評議會的權力運用是否合法。
#### 結語
我提出上面的批評,不外是向關洪兩位同學指出兩點:(一)不要在香港未來政治安排這問題上,再存有任何幻想。「前途由我主宰」固然是動聽感人 ,但並不切實可行。(二)罵人可以, 但要有理。對於在香港前途上提倡國家民族利益的人,不要亂打棍子,盲駕瞎打,不是有知識的人所應做的。

### Perplexity | Aug 2026 | Translation

#### Two Points for Discussion on Guan Yongxiang and Hong Yisheng’s “Five Million Hong Kongers Decide Their Own Future”

**By Guo Shi Gu Ren**

Issue 4 of _Xueyuan_ placed the article “Five Million Hong Kongers Decide Their Own Future” by two fellow students, Guan Yongxiang and Hong Yisheng, on the front page with prominent typography. If the editors did this to commend the authors’ courage in publishing their views, it might barely be justifiable—after all, Guan and Hong openly lashed out at both the Chinese and British governments in their text, which indeed required a certain boldness. Let us hope it was not merely a momentary impulse! Regrettably, the content of this article is grossly mismatched with its eye-catching “packaging.” The authors’ perspectives and analysis are overly simplistic; there is more than enough emotional incitement, but far too little persuasion by reason. Below, I discuss two major questionable points in the text.

#### Is Hong Konger Self-Determination Feasible?

First, the authors propose the political claim of “five million Hong Kongers deciding their own future,” yet they do not even analyze the feasibility of this claim, let alone mention any ideas for its implementation. Of course, no one would expect Guan and Hong to discuss these two issues in detail in an article under 2,000 characters. However, they should understand that in many people’s minds, this claim is nearly a fantasy, an utterly impractical proposition. Under such circumstances, completely avoiding the feasibility of Hong Konger self-determination and methods of implementation is undoubtedly a serious omission. If the authors do not answer these two questions, what practical significance is there in exhaustively crying out for Hong Kongers to decide their own future?

Regarding Hong Kong’s future political arrangements, the Chinese and British governments should of course respect the will of the local five million residents, since any arrangement will directly affect the well-being of these five million people. Among various considerations, the will of Hong Kong residents ought to occupy a priority position. This is something believers in democracy and freedom regard as self-evident. But on the other hand, if we adopt a pragmatic, truth-seeking attitude, we cannot but admit that Hong Kong’s future political arrangements cannot, at present or in the foreseeable future, be controlled by local residents. This is not pouring cold water, nor is it speaking without backbone or lacking ambition; it is a well-grounded conclusion.

#### The Political Culture of Hong Kongers

First, a mentality of political apathy and fear still普遍 and deeply exists among the broad masses of citizens. The general public has not yet awakened to the fact that they have a voice in future political arrangements, let alone any determination to defend their preferred way of life. For the vast majority of citizens, “1997” may be a “synonym” for unemployment and economic downturn; a distant and abstract issue; an inexplicable fear; a joke for after-dinner conversation. In short, it is not a crisis to be faced and responded to with united effort.

About half a year ago, when the two power companies announced price hikes amid soaring prices of all goods and difficult living conditions, some pressure groups formed the “Joint Committee for Monitoring Public Utilities” and launched a “Freeze the Two Power Companies’ Price Hikes” signature campaign, collecting over 500,000 signatures. Yet the authorities were not “moved” and continued as before. The reason for such a response from the authorities is simple: those 500,000 signatures were really just 500,000 signatures—nothing more. They did not include any determination for further action, so they could not pose a real threat to the rulers. To control our own future destiny, the price we must pay is far more than just signing names! If we are honest with ourselves, in terms of political culture development, we are still at the “drinking milk” stage!

The transformation of political culture is not an overnight matter; for Hong Kongers to rise and fight for their rights is indeed “a long, long road ahead”!

#### Constraints of the Political Structure

Besides the insufficiency of political culture, Hong Kong’s political structure is another constraint. We simply do not have a system that, on important public affairs, can reflect, judge, and interpret the intentions of people from all social strata, and compel the government to consider the needs and interests of all strata in the decision-making process, taking the intentions of the majority as the guide under the principle of respecting each individual’s rights. To put it more explicitly: because we lack a legislative body composed of publicly elected representatives, on the major issue of Hong Kong’s future political arrangements—which involves the rights and interests of every citizen—there is simply no one qualified to speak and make judgments on behalf of Hong Kong citizens.

Can the members of the Executive and Legislative Councils do this? No. This is not only because all council members are appointed by the government, and most do not belong to the lower classes, but more importantly, because their consistent performance has been to endorse the government more than to plead for the people on major issues. Then, can the Hong Kong representatives to the Chinese National People’s Congress and the Chinese People’s Political Consultative Conference do this? Of course not. I believe that among ten ordinary citizens, at most two or three know what the NPC and CPPCC are. How can we then say that these “representatives” truly represent us?

Moreover, on the issue of Hong Kong’s future, citizens’ intentions may be highly divergent. On one hand, there are entrepreneurs like Li Pengfei who oppose “Hong Kongers ruling Hong Kong”; on the other hand, there are people like On Zi-chieh who hold the opposite view. On one hand, there are interpretations of citizens’ will by the _South China Morning Post_, _The Economic Journal_, and _Oriental Daily_; on the other hand, there are expressions of citizens’ sentiments by _Wen Wei Po_ and _Ta Kung Pao_. The results of surveys conducted by various social groups also do not show that citizens’ intentions are unified on Hong Kong’s future political arrangements and related important issues. Faced with such a complex issue as Hong Kong’s future, our existing political structure appears utterly incompetent, unable to balance the needs and interests of all social strata!

Finally, we must also consider time constraints. Broadly speaking, the Chinese and British sides must reach an agreement by the end of next year or earlier, because if dragged out further, the already shaky confidence of Hong Kongers will be very difficult to maintain. In such a rushed timeframe, Hong Kongers’ political culture and political structure cannot undergo essential changes. Moreover, when taking any action, we must maintain local social integration and stability, avoiding widespread fear and unrest.

Although the above analysis is limited to internal factors within Hong Kong society and does not touch upon the key issue of the Chinese government’s determination to resume sovereignty over Hong Kong, I believe it is already sufficient to show that the claim of “five million Hong Kongers deciding their own future” is an IQ-zero, utterly impractical idea in realpolitik.

#### No Need for Excessive Despair—Organize a Grand Alliance

Although Hong Konger self-determination is a fantasy, I absolutely do not believe that our destiny is entirely at the mercy of others. No matter how many constraints or how great the difficulties, there is always some room for us to exert influence—no matter how limited that influence may be. We have not yet reached a point of complete helplessness.

Mr. Walter Loh has previously proposed forming a grand alliance composed of various industrial, commercial, cultural, religious, and social groups to express Hong Kongers’ will. This is a relatively feasible method under current constraints. The student union or the tertiary sector could also consider initiating an organization like a “Joint Conference of All Sectors Concerned about Hong Kong’s Future.” For matters that benefit the well-being of five million people, we should step forward without hesitation.

#### Guan and Hong Are Shooting Without a Target

Having discussed the feasibility of Hong Konger self-determination, I now turn to another issue for discussion with Guan Yongxiang and Hong Yisheng. They state: “Nation and ethnicity, hanging in our mouths, are merely empty terms. I do not believe, and furthermore will not accept, that one can abandon one’s own dignity for the so-called ‘national future’ or ‘national interests.’ I may decide to orient my choices by ‘national future’ or ‘national interests,’ but I cannot impose these on others. Human dignity is built on the ability of a person to decide and strive for their own needs; no one else can decide for them. If there is such a thing, it is oppression.”

The authors believe that people may use “national future” and “national interests” as guides for their own choices, but cannot impose these value orientations on others; otherwise, it is oppression. If someone insists on imposing their value orientations on others and forcing them to accept, that is certainly immoral—a manifestation of disregarding human dignity. But the immorality of this behavior does not lie in the content of the value orientation forcibly imposed on others. Even if it were “opposing national and ethnic interests,” it would equally be immoral if forcibly imposed. So why do Guan and Hong single out “national future” and “national interests” as things not to be imposed on others? Is it because most people who advocate viewing Hong Kong’s future from the perspective of national and ethnic interests are imposing their own value orientations and standards of right and wrong on others? If not, then the above statement by Guan and Hong is shooting without a target. If yes, then Guan and Hong are clearly distorting facts, speaking irresponsibly, or are confused in their thinking and concepts. Why do I say this?

First, in a free society like Hong Kong, everyone has the right to make choices regarding Hong Kong’s future political arrangements according to their own value standards, and to urge others to follow their standards. Guan and Hong probably would not object to this proposition, would they? On personal matters, such as food choices, each person naturally follows their own preferences and goes their own way; there is no need to demand others follow their tastes. But once it involves matters between people—for example, how to coexist and cooperate—and if people’s value standards are inconsistent, then whose standard should be followed, A’s or B’s? In such cases, people will inevitably try to persuade each other to认同 their own set, or compromise with each other, or invite a third party to arbitrate. These several ways of handling the situation are all natural and reasonable. Since Hong Kong’s future political arrangements are not purely a personal matter, and everyone’s views are inconsistent, what is wrong with each person urging and persuading others to认同 their own set—whether that set is national and ethnic interests, personal interests, or even British Empire interests? There is nothing wrong with doing so.

Second, unless violence or the threat of violence is used, employing legal means—such as expressing opinions in newspapers or speaking at rallies—to promote value concepts like “ethnic interests” or “national future” simply does not constitute imposition or oppression.

Since promoting ethnic unity and national interests regarding Hong Kong’s future is neither impermissible nor can it be said to forcibly impose these value orientations on others, what practical significance is there in Guan and Hong’s above statement: “I may decide to orient my choices by ‘national future’ and ‘national interests,’ but I cannot impose these on others… If there is such a thing, it is oppression”?

If Guan and Hong’s accusation is directed at the Student Union Executive Committee and the Council, then the key issue is whether their decisions are within the powers granted by the constitution and passed through legal procedures.
